# Sound

How a finished draft sounds. Run this pass over a draft that already satisfies Part 0 of `rules.md`, because it changes phrasing and not facts.

**`humanizer.md` beside this file is the rule set.** Read it. It is vendored verbatim from https://github.com/blader/humanizer under MIT, 25 numbered patterns with their watch-for lists and before/after pairs, and its patterns come from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing". Do not work from a summary of it, including this file: an earlier version of this guide compressed it to a handful of bullets and the reports still read as generated, twice.

This file holds only what that rule set does not know about an analysis report.

## Run its process, not a find-and-replace

The four steps in `humanizer.md` under "How to work" are the pass. Two of them are the ones that get skipped:

- **Read the draft aloud.** Nothing else catches monotone.
- **Write the final version by stating each point naturally, instead of patching flagged phrases one at a time.** A draft repaired phrase by phrase keeps the shape that made it sound generated.

Then search for the five tells that most often survive a rewrite: a not-X-but-Y contrast, a one-line closer, a dash, a triad, a bold label.

## Severity, and the trap in it

§1 to §5 justify an edit on one sighting. A pattern marked *weak alone* needs other tells in the same passage before you act. Do not sweep a whole document for a *weak alone* pattern and change every instance, because that is rhythm by rule again, in the other direction.

## Vary sentence length

`humanizer.md` says it in one line under "How to work": real writing alternates short and long. A uniform sentence length reads as machine output whether the number is 40 words or 20, so **do not impose a word cap**. An earlier version of this file set one at 30 words and produced a report where every sentence was the same size.

What actually goes wrong in a report is a single sentence carrying a claim, its denominator, its trap and its citation at once. That is four ideas, and rule 0.5 already says one idea per sentence. Split it because of 0.5, then let the resulting sentences be whatever lengths they turn out to be.

The same applies to paragraphs. Rule 0.6 asks for one job per paragraph; a paragraph holding five findings fails 0.6 and is five paragraphs or a table. No word count enters into it.

## What a report keeps that the rule set would cut

- **A derivation's trap** is not §9 stacked qualifiers. It reports real doubt the source supports, which §9 explicitly keeps.
- **An inference marked as one** is not §23 a knowledge-limit disclaimer. Saying which records would settle a question is the opposite of filling a gap with a guess.
- **Naming what the logs cannot tell you** is a section a report owes its reader, not a §13 stock "challenges and outlook" ending.

Where a report genuinely cannot support a claim, §23 still applies in full: say what the records do not show, and never present a guess as a fact.

## Where a report fails on its own

- **§24, from a rule of ours.** Rule 8.1 asks a heading to state its finding. The moment it does, the paragraph beneath it tends to state the finding again before reaching any evidence. A finding-heading is followed by the evidence for it.
- **§19 through the evidence markers.** Bolding a whole table row, or every category name, is decoration. Rule 8.8 wants one row marked, not seven.
- **§1 through the tiers.** Distinguishing measured from derived, activity from value, unknown from zero invites `rather than` and `, not` into every other sentence. Each is defensible alone; six in a 45-line body is the strongest tell in the draft. Keep the contrast only where it corrects something the reader believes.
